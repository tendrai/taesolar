How much does solar cost in Phuket in 2026?

Nobody enjoys asking for a price and being told "it depends". It genuinely does depend — but there are real, published ranges for installed solar in Thailand, and you deserve to see them before anyone visits your roof. Here is what systems actually cost, what drives the difference, and where the cheap quotes quietly cut corners.

What solar systems cost in Thailand in 2026

These are the installed price ranges we see across the Thai market this year — equipment, mounting, electrical work and labour included. They are wide on purpose, because a 5 kW system on a single-storey metal roof and a 5 kW system on a three-storey tiled villa are not the same job.

If a quote sits well below the bottom of these ranges, that is not automatically a scam — but it is a question worth asking. Something has to give, and it is usually the mounting hardware, the inverter, or the electrical protection you cannot see from the ground.

Why bigger systems cost less per kW

The cost per kilowatt falls steadily as systems get larger — from roughly ฿30,000–57,000 per kW at 3 kW down to about ฿20,000–26,000 per kW at 50 kW. The reason is simple: a lot of the work is fixed regardless of size. Getting a team and equipment to your property, running the main cable to your consumer unit, doing the electrical connection and commissioning the system costs about the same whether you fit eight panels or eighty.

In practice this means very small systems are poor value per unit generated. If your roof and your usage can support it, going from 3 kW to 5 kW usually costs far less than the extra capacity suggests.

Size to your usage, not your roof. A bigger array only pays if you use the electricity yourself. Filling a roof with panels for a household that is out all day is an expensive way to donate power to the grid at a low buyback rate.

What adding a battery does to the price

Roughly speaking, adding storage doubles the cost. A 5 kW system with a battery typically runs ฿300,000–400,000 rather than ฿130,000–250,000. A 10 kW system with a 14 kWh battery lands around ฿400,000.

That premium buys two things: the ability to use daytime generation in the evening, and the ability to keep essential circuits alive during a power cut. Both are genuinely useful, but a battery almost always lengthens payback rather than shortening it. If your goal is to cut your bill as quickly as possible, a grid-tied system without storage remains the better financial decision. If your circuit drops out often, or your usage is concentrated after dark, storage starts to earn its keep.

Where the money actually goes

For a mid-range 5 kW system at around ฿180,000, the split looks roughly like this:

  • Panels — about 47%. The largest single line, and the one buyers focus on most.
  • Inverter — about 22%. The component that does the hard work, and the one most likely to need replacing during the system's life.
  • Mounting and wiring — about 13%. Rails, clamps, fixings, DC and AC cable, isolators and protection.
  • Labour — about 12%. Survey, installation, electrical connection and commissioning.
  • Permits and fees — about 6%. Paperwork, applications and administration.

Worth noticing: panels are less than half the bill. When somebody undercuts the market by ฿40,000, they cannot get there on panel choice alone. The savings come out of the other 53% — the parts that determine whether the system is still working properly in year twelve.

What that buys you: tariffs and Phuket sunshine

Two numbers decide whether the spend makes sense.

The first is what you currently pay. Thai residential electricity has averaged about ฿3.95 per kWh for May–August 2026 including the Ft adjustment, before VAT. Add 7% VAT and the blended effective cost is roughly ฿4.23 per kWh. For reference, the January–April 2026 average was ฿3.88 — so the tariff moves, and not always in your favour.

The second is how much your roof will actually generate. Typical Thai yield is 1,300–1,500 kWh per installed kWp per year, or about 3.6–4.1 kWh per kWp per day. Southern Thailand — Phuket very much included — sits at the lower end of that band because of how much rain we get, so any estimate built on the national average is flattering you.

Planning on the conservative figure, a 5 kWp array at 1,300 kWh per kWp is around 6,500 kWh a year. Valued at ฿4.23, that is a shade under ฿27,500 a year — but only if you use every unit yourself. Whatever you export instead earns considerably less, which is why your daily routine matters as much as your roof does.

We will tell you when solar isn't worth it. If your usage is mostly in the evening, your roof is heavily shaded, or your bill is small, the honest answer may be "not yet". We would rather say that than sell you a system you regret.

The PEA/MEA household buyback scheme

If you want to be paid for surplus power rather than simply giving it away, the 2026 household rooftop scheme is the route. The terms are fixed and worth knowing before you size a system:

  • Buyback rate of ฿2.20 per kWh — around half what you pay to buy a unit back.
  • Open to residential (Type 1) users only.
  • Maximum 5 kW AC per meter.
  • 10-year contract term.
  • Applications run 1 July 2026 to 30 November 2027.
  • ฿2,000 application fee, excluding VAT.
  • Roof-mounted systems only — no ground mounts.
  • The programme is capped at 500 MW nationally.

The gap between ฿4.23 to buy and ฿2.20 to sell is the most important number in this article. Self-consumption is where the value is; export is a consolation prize rather than a business model. It is also why we do not recommend oversizing an array on the assumption that the grid will pay for it.

Why two quotes for the same roof differ

Legitimate price variation usually comes from a handful of physical realities:

Roof type and condition

Metal sheet roofs are quick. Concrete tile takes longer and needs careful waterproofing at every penetration. An older roof may need attention before anything is bolted to it — which is exactly what a survey is for.

Coastal corrosion resistance

Phuket is an island. Salt air attacks standard fixings, and mounting hardware specified for an inland site will not last as long here. Corrosion-resistant rails, clamps and fasteners cost more up front. This is one of the most common places for a cheap quote to save money, and one of the most expensive places to be wrong.

Inverter brand and specification

Inverters vary enormously in price, warranty terms, monitoring quality and — crucially — whether anyone in Thailand can service them in five years. A quote that names the exact brand and model lets you compare. A quote that says "high-efficiency inverter" does not.

Cable runs

If the consumer unit sits directly under the array, the DC and AC runs are short. If the panels are on a far roof section, or the meter is at the other end of a long villa, you pay for cable, conduit, labour and the voltage-drop compensation that comes with distance.

Access and scaffolding

A single-storey bungalow with a driveway alongside is straightforward. A three-storey villa on a slope with no vehicle access needs scaffolding, more hands and more time. This is not padding — it is the difference between a safe install and an unsafe one.

What cheap quotes leave out

When a number looks too good, it is usually because something has been left off the page rather than genuinely done cheaper. The recurring omissions:

  • Named equipment. "Tier 1 panels" is not a specification. Brand, model and wattage should be in writing.
  • Electrical protection. Surge protection, correctly rated isolators, proper earthing and any consumer unit work needed to connect safely.
  • Coastal-grade mounting. See above — the cheapest fixings look identical on day one.
  • Scaffolding and access. Frequently quoted as an "extra" once the team arrives and sees the property.
  • Permits and application fees. Including the ฿2,000 scheme application fee if you intend to export.
  • Commissioning, monitoring and handover. A system that generates but that you cannot monitor is a system you will not notice failing.
  • A workmanship warranty, and someone to call. Manufacturer warranties on panels mean little if the company that fitted them has closed.

The fair comparison between two quotes is not the bottom line. It is the bottom line once both quotes contain the same scope of work.

Working out your own number

You can get surprisingly close on your own: take your monthly bill, work out how much of your usage happens between roughly 9am and 4pm, and apply the conservative Phuket yield figure to the array size your roof can carry.
